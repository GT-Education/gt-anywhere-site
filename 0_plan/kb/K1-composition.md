## 1. Question

What makes a page read as **designed** rather than assembled: grid systems, vertical rhythm, hierarchy, measure, whitespace, Gestalt grouping, the Swiss and editorial traditions, and the idea of the first screen as a thesis. And: how do the best institutional sites actually compose a first screen and a section ladder?

### 2b. Hierarchy, grouping and the measured behaviour of readers

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F15 | Visual hierarchy is "the organization of the design elements on the page so that the eye is guided to consume each design element in the order of intended importance." Concrete limits given: "Use no more than 3 sizes — small, medium, and large"; "Limit how many elements are big to a maximum of 2"; body 14–16px, subheader 18–22px, header up to 32px; and for colour, 2 primary + 2 secondary, "no more than 3 contrast variations" | Kelley Gordon, NN/g, 17 Jan 2021, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/ |
| F16 | The five visual-design principles NN/g teaches are scale, visual hierarchy, balance, contrast and Gestalt. Contrast is defined as "the juxtaposition of visually dissimilar elements in order to convey the fact that these elements are different… have different functions, behave differently" | Kelley Gordon, NN/g, 1 Mar 2020 (reviewed 1 Sep 2026), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/ |
| F17 | Proximity beats other cues: "Proximity is one of the most important grouping principles and can overpower competing visual cues such as similarity of color or shape." And: "Using varying amounts of whitespace to either unite or separate elements is key to communicating meaningful groupings" | Aurora Harley, NN/g, 2 Aug 2020, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-proximity/ |
| F18 | A boundary beats proximity: "items within a boundary are perceived as a group"; a clear boundary is "a strong visual cue that can overpower other grouping principles such as proximity or similarity." But over-using borders and backgrounds risks "visual clutter and false floors that discourage user scrolling" — exhaust whitespace first | Aurora Harley, NN/g, 12 Jul 2020, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/common-region/ |
| F19 | Gestalt in one line: "The whole is other than the sum of the parts" (Koffka). Figure/ground: "Elements are perceived as either figure (the element in focus) or ground (the background on which the figure rests)" — a page with no stable figure/ground has no composition | Steven Bradley, Smashing Magazine, 29 Mar 2014, https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2014/03/design-principles-visual-perception-and-the-principles-of-gestalt/ |
| F20 | Attention above the fold, measured: users spend "about 57% of their page-viewing time above the fold"; "74% of the viewing time was spent in the first two screenfuls, up to 2160px"; "more than 42% of the viewing time fell within the top 20% of the page"; "more than 65% of the viewing time above the fold was concentrated in the top half." Recommendation: "Reserve the top of the page for high-priority content… Keep major CTAs above the fold" | Therese Fessenden, NN/g, 15 Apr 2018, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/ |
| F21 | The fold's effect size: content just above the fold received "102% more views" than content 100px below it; Google's advertising data showed 73% viewability above versus 44% below; NN/g's synthesis is "84% is the average difference in how users treat info above vs. below the fold." Scrollability should be signalled by compelling content, not arrows | Amy Schade, NN/g, 1 Feb 2015, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/ |
| F22 | The F-pattern: "First lines of text on a page receive more gazes than subsequent lines" and "first few words on the left of each line of text receive more fixations." Users "may skip important content simply because it appears on the right side of the page" and will not know they did. Remedy: front-load headings and first paragraphs with the informative words | Kara Pernice, NN/g, 12 Nov 2017 (reviewed 19 Aug 2026), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/ |
| F23 | Users scan: "79 percent of our test users always scanned any new page… only 16 percent read word-by-word." Measured usability gains over a control page: concise text +58%, scannable layout +47%, objective (non-promotional) language +27%, all three combined +124%. Users "detested 'marketese'" | Jakob Nielsen, NN/g, 30 Sep 1997, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/ |
| F24 | Chunking is a layout instrument, not just a writing one: keep text lines to 50–75 characters, use white space between short paragraphs, and group with headings, background colours, rules and proximity. Miller's ~7 chunks applies to what must be **held in memory**, not to a continuously visible menu | Kate Moran, NN/g, 20 Mar 2016, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chunking/ |
| F25 | Decision cost rises with option count: "The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices" (Hick and Hyman, 1952). Takeaway used here: reduce choices where speed matters, and highlight a preferred option only when there is one | Jon Yablonski, Laws of UX, https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/ (no page date) |
| F26 | Information scent is what a link promises: "the user's imperfect estimate of the value that the source will deliver." Vague labels such as "More" or "Learn more" have low scent for every information need; images must be descriptive rather than decorative; landing pages must "provide sufficient context quickly" | Raluca Budiu, NN/g, 2 Feb 2020, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/ |
| F27 | Attractiveness is read as competence: "Users are more tolerant of minor usability issues when they find an interface visually appealing." Kurosu and Kashimura (1995), 26 ATM interface variants and 252 participants, found aesthetic appeal correlated more strongly with *perceived* ease of use than with *actual* ease of use | Kate Moran, NN/g, 3 Feb 2024 (reviewed 1 Sep 2026), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/ |
| F28 | Audience-based ("who are you?") navigation has five named failure modes: users identify with more than one group or none; "about" versus "for" is ambiguous; "forcing people to self-identify creates an additional step and takes people out of their task mindset"; users fear the information they see is incomplete; and duplicated content causes pogo-sticking. **It works when** categories are "mutually exclusive", in "plain-language terms", and each has enough unique content "to justify its own section" | Katie Sherwin, NN/g, 7 Sep 2015, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/audience-based-navigation/ |
| F29 | On phones, comprehension is roughly equal (about 3 percentage points higher on mobile) but **effort** is higher for hard material: readers spent about 30ms longer per word on difficult articles on mobile. Prioritisation matters more on small screens, and complex material needs its own mobile testing | Kate Moran, NN/g, 11 Dec 2016, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/ |

### 2c. Measure, rhythm and whitespace

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F30 | Measure: "anything from 45 to 75 characters is widely regarded as a satisfactory length of line"; 66 characters is the single-column ideal for serifed text; 40–50 for multi-column | Richard Rutter, *The Elements of Typographic Style Applied to the Web*, §2.1.2, applying Robert Bringhurst, https://webtypography.net/2.1.2 |
| F31 | Body text numbers: "In print, the most comfortable range for body text is 10–12 point. On the web, 15–25 pixels." Line spacing "should be 120–145% of the point size." | F32 | A government-scale default: "we recommend using either a 'two-thirds' or a 'two-thirds and one-third' layout. | F33 | A measure axiom stated in CSS terms: "the measure should never exceed `60ch`", expressed in `ch` rather than pixels so it survives a font-size change; design axioms exist because "your output will be inconsistent and malformed" without them; the designer should be "the browser's mentor, rather than its micro-manager" (Allsopp, 2000) | Heydon Pickering and Andy Bell, *Every Layout*, Axioms, https://every-layout.dev/rudiments/axioms/ |
| F34 | Macro versus micro whitespace: macro is "the space between major elements in a composition", micro is "the space between smaller elements: between list items, between a caption and an image, or between words and letters." | F35 | Modular scales give harmony that arbitrary numbers do not; a worked golden-ratio example from an 18px base yields 29.124px and 44.856px. | F36 | Vertical rhythm is a pattern-recognition argument: "the more different shapes there are for the brain to analyze, the longer it's going to take." | F37 | Fluid scales are defined by two endpoints, not by breakpoints: define a small-screen scale, define a large-screen scale, "tell the browser to interpolate between the two scales, based on the current viewport width." | F38 | Sensible inherited defaults from a widely used reset: body `line-height: 1.5`, headings and interactive elements `line-height: 1.1`, `text-size-adjust: none`, `box-sizing: border-box` | Andy Bell, 18 Sep 2023, https://piccalil.li/blog/a-modern-css-reset/ |
| F39 | Institutional grid arithmetic in public use: a 12-column grid, default gutter of 2 spacing units rising to 4 units (32px) at desktop, container default 1024px, breakpoints mobile-lg 480px, tablet 640px, desktop 1024px, desktop-lg 1200px, widescreen 1400px | U.S. Web Design System, layout grid, updated 6 Nov 2024, https://designsystem.digital.gov/utilities/layout-grid/ |

### 2d. Composing for the web's actual material

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F40 | The web's material property is edgelessness: designing for "an edgeless surface of unknown proportions comprised of small, individual, and variable elements from multiple vantages assembled into a readable whole." ### 2f. "The first screen as thesis": what the evidence supports, and what it does not

The plan names this idea. **No fetched source states it as a named doctrine**, so it is recorded here as a synthesis with its parts shown, not as a citation (see OQ-5). What the parts do support:

| Claim in the idea | Supported by | Not supported |
|---|---|---|
| The top of the page is where the argument has to be made | F20: 57% of viewing time above the fold, and >65% of *that* in the top half. F21: content just above the fold got 102% more views than content 100px below | It does not follow that everything must be above the fold; F20's own recommendation is a scrolling page with priorities ordered |
| The thesis must be a **proposition**, not a mood | F52: a homepage must "communicate who you are and what you do" with a tagline that "explicitly conveys what your site or company does". F3/F26: meaning first, then the specific link | Nothing supports the corollary that a hero image can carry the proposition. F26 is the opposite: images must be descriptive, not decorative |
| It must be readable in a scan, not a read | F22: first lines and first words get the fixations. F23: 79% scan; front-loaded, objective, scannable copy measured +124% | No source supports compressing the thesis to a slogan; F23's gain came from *concision*, not from brevity for its own sake |
| Its visual form is one big thing against quiet | F13: scale contrast is the strongest cheap move. F15: at most two big elements. F2: reduction inside a grid produces "intelligibility, and clarity" | Vignelli separates strength from impact (F13): a large element is not automatically a thesis |
| It sets the syntax for everything below | F8: consistency is "the appropriate relationship of the various syntactical elements… how the parts relate to the whole" | — |

The editorial tradition sits behind all of it: the Swiss line (F10) supplies the asymmetric grid, flush-left setting and objective photography; the editorial line supplies the scale jump (F13's *Books* spread, where one word holds the whole spread); and the web's own literature (F45) says the medium is mostly type, so the thesis is typographic before it is pictorial. That is the intellectual case for a **typographic** first screen at GT, and it is why OQ-7 asks for one photograph-free direction.

## 3. Principles the studio adopts

| # | Principle | Traces to |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | **Declare the grid before composing (Option, R15).** A mock that adopts a grid states its columns, gutter, page margin and container per breakpoint in its rationale, and aligns every section to it — a grid that is not written down is not a grid. R15: a proposed grid is an option a brief may adopt, not a mandate; the rubric scores the outcome, not conformity to a written grid | F1, F3, F5, F39 |
| P2 | **One thesis, two paths, one big thing.** The first screen carries exactly one element at the top type step and at most two "big" elements; any third competing path moves below the fold | F15, F20, F25, F55, F58 |
| P3 | **Hierarchy by space, weight and alignment before size.** Size differences are spent sparingly; the rest of the hierarchy comes from whitespace, weight, surface and alignment | F12, F13, F15, F16, F34 |
| P4 | **Group with space first, boundaries second.** Inside a group the space is small; between groups it is large; a border or tint is added only when stacking makes the whitespace ambiguous, and never heavily enough to read as a false floor | F17, F18, F19, F24 |
| P5 | **Measure governs every text block.** No running text exceeds 75 characters at any viewport in the matrix; prose targets 45–75; display lines target roughly 30–45. The constraint is expressed in `ch`, not pixels | F30, F31, F32, F33 |
| P6 | **Rhythm is a multiple, not a feeling (Option, R15).** A mock that adopts this makes every vertical measure a multiple of 8px drawn from the token scale, body content holding the rhythm while peripheral elements may break it. R15: the 8px rule is an option a brief may adopt, not a shared mandate | F7, F36, F38 |
| P7 | **Compose fluidly against two endpoints, and against height as well as width.** Type and space interpolate between a small-screen and a large-screen scale; first-screen fit is solved with `svh` plus a `max-height` companion query, never with rem heights tuned to one viewport | F37, F40, F41, F42, F43, F44 |
| P8 | **Front-load everything.** The first words of the h1, of each section heading and of each link carry the information; nothing load-bearing sits to the right of the primary reading column | F22, F23, F26, F51 |
| P9 | **Objective over promotional, always.** Concise, scannable and objective language is worth more than persuasive language, and every figure that appears must be attributable — the composition should have a place to put the attribution | F23, F52, F58 |
| P10 | **Craft is the argument.** Detail is not decoration: a page that is visibly cared for is read as an institution that is competent. Optical alignment, no orphans, one radius set, restraint in motion | F9, F13, F14, F27, F47, F48, F49 |

## 5. How it applies to GT

### 5a. The grid and rhythm for the routing landing page (Option, R15)

Derived from F3, F5, F30–F33, F39 and the existing `tokens.css` (container 90rem, narrow 48rem, 4px space ladder). Per R15 this grid is one option a brief may adopt, not a studio-wide mandate; the rubric scores the first-screen outcome, not conformity to this table.

| Breakpoint | Columns | Gutter | Page margin | Content width | Column width |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 360–767 | 4 | `--space-4` 16px | `--space-5` 24px | 312–719px | 66–168px |
| 768–1023 | 8 | `--space-5` 24px | `--space-6` 32px | 704–959px | ~67–99px |
| 1024–1439 | 12 | `--space-6` 32px | `--space-7` 48px | 928–1343px | ~48–83px |
| ≥1440 | 12 | `--space-6` 32px | `--space-8` 64px | 1312px (container capped at `--container-max` 90rem) | **80px exactly** |

At 1440 the arithmetic is clean and worth fixing as the reference: `(1312 − 11 × 32) ÷ 12 = 80px`. A mock that adopts this table states it (or its own deliberate departure) in its rationale.

The 4 / 8 / 12 laddering is an **interpretation**, not a sourced value: F39 gives 12 columns at every width, and F32 gives proportional fractions rather than a count. Twelve columns at 390 would yield a 6.5px column, which is meaningless, so the ladder is our judgement. Logged in section 7 as OQ-4.

**Vertical rhythm (Option, R15).** Base unit **8px** (F36; every step of GT's space ladder from `--space-3` up is already a multiple of 8). A mock that adopts this makes every **block gap** — the space between elements and between a section's heading and its body — a multiple of 8 taken from the token scale, with `--space-1` (2px) and `--space-2` (4px) reserved for micro-space inside a component (F34's micro whitespace). R15: this is a brief's option, not a shared mandate.

**Section padding is the one exemption**, because it is fluid rather than stepped (P7): `padding-block: clamp(4rem, 2rem + 5vw, 8rem)` resolves to 64px at 390 and 104px at 1440 — the endpoints sit on the ladder (`--space-8` to `--space-11`) but intermediate widths will not land on 8. That is deliberate and is the only place the multiple-of-8 test is skipped.

**Measure.** `--container-narrow` is **48rem = 768px**, which at the 16px body size is roughly **90–96 characters** depending on the face — above every source's ceiling (F30 75, F31 90, F32 75, F33 60). Consequence: `--container-narrow` is a **section** container, never a text measure. Prose blocks get their own constraint, `max-width: 65ch` (about 34rem in Literata), and the QA matrix gets a test that no text node exceeds 75 characters at any viewport. Flagged for the register as OQ-2.

**The h1 clamp.** The umbrella sentence is fixed by `copy.md` at 22 words / ~134 characters. The existing `--text-3xl` is 3.25rem (52px), which at 390px with 24px margins gives roughly 13 characters per line — about 10 lines and roughly 600px of type before anything else is drawn. That alone breaks the 390×664 in-app viewport. The fix modeled here is a fluid clamp that reaches 52px only on large screens:

```css
h1 { font-size: clamp(1.75rem, 1.25rem + 2.22vw, 3.25rem); line-height: 1.15; }
@media (min-width: 1024px) and (max-height: 800px) {
  h1 { font-size: clamp(1.75rem, 1.3rem + 1.6vw, 2.5rem); }
}
```

Computed (verified by arithmetic, not by eye): **28.0px at 360**, **28.7px at 390**, 37.1px at 768, 48.4px at 1280 when the window is tall, **40.0px at 1280×720** via the height query, and **52.0px at 1440** — the existing `--text-3xl` maximum, preserved exactly. **This 52px ceiling is this cluster's own proposal, not the studio default: per R5 (`kb/KB-RESOLUTIONS.md`), K2's clamp — topping at 46px — is the working default, and a brief that instead adopts this 52px clamp is taking a DD-19 departure (DD-38 is the pending register row that decides between them).**

There is no universal line-count quota (R5): fit is measured on the fixed copy at every matrix row (`17` section 4), not asserted in advance. QA test: no one-word last line in the h1 at 390, 768, 1280 or 1440 (an orphan check, not a line-count quota); actual line count is read off the rendered page per row — this file's own 1440 model in section 5b runs the h1 to 4 lines, which is exactly why a fixed universal quota does not hold across breakpoints.

**A scale problem to resolve.** `--text-3xl` (3.25rem) and the h2 value (3rem) are 8% apart — below the threshold at which a reader registers a rank difference, and in tension with F15's "no more than 3 sizes". Recommendation (a proposal for the register, not a rule — see the follow-up rows at the end of this file): on the landing, use 3.25rem for the h1 only and drop section h2s to `--text-2xl` (2rem) at ≥1024, giving a clean 52 / 32 / 20 / 16 ladder — three sizes plus the body, exactly F15. Logged as OQ-3.

### 5b. How a two-door first screen should be composed

The doors survive F28's five objections only because the categories are mutually exclusive ("in person" / "online"), plain-language, and each has a whole site of unique content behind it. Two obligations follow from that same finding: the two doors must look **equal** (A3), and the page must immediately answer "am I missing something?" — which is what the compare anchor is for, and why it must sit within the first two screenfuls (F20, 74% of viewing time up to 2160px).

**At 390×664 (V3 in `17` section 4) — one worked example. V2 (375×629) is the matrix's actual binding fold case and is checked separately below; this file originally checked only V3 and mislabeled it "the binding constraint."**

| Order | Element | Size | Space below |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Header: logo left, "Current families" and "Contact admissions" right. **Not sticky** — a sticky bar spends the scarcest resource on the page | 56px | — |
| 2 | h1, umbrella sentence, flush left, full 342px content width | 28.7px / 1.15, ≤5 lines ≈ 165px | `--space-4` 16px |
| 3 | Provenance line, once, muted | 14px ≈ 18px box | `--space-6` 32px — the **active whitespace** (F34), and the largest gap on the screen |
| 4 | Door A: eyebrow "In person" (12px Inconsolata, uppercase) · name "Georgetown Campus" (20px Literata) with a 48px round photo inset beside it · a 4–6 word scent line · full-width CTA (≥44px) | ~176px | `--space-4` 16px |
| 5 | Door B: identical structure, "Online" / "GT Anywhere" | ~176px | `--space-4` 16px |
| 6 | Compare anchor line, 14px, links to `#compare` | ~20px | — |

**What `17` section 4 actually requires above the fold**: the header, the h1 (umbrella sentence), the provenance line, and **both door CTAs** — rows 1–5 above, through the bottom of Door B. The compare anchor (row 6) is not required above the fold; F21's own point is that a half-visible promise of more content is the correct signal to scroll, so row 6 landing below the line is not a defect.

**Recomputed against the matrix (R5 — measured fit beats estimated arithmetic).** Rows 1–5 sum to 56 + 165 + 16 + 18 + 32 + 176 + 16 + 176 = **655px** of required content.

| Row | Viewport | Fold limit (height − 24px) | 655px required content | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V3 | 390×664 | 664 − 24 = **640px** | 655 > 640 | **Fails by 15px** |
| V2 | 375×629 (the matrix's binding fold case) | 629 − 24 = **605px** | 655 > 605 | **Fails by 50px** |

This is an **estimated** budget built from assumed type metrics and component paddings, not a rendered measurement, so per R5 it cannot be marked ✓ either way — but as estimated, it clears neither row, and it is further from clearing the binding V2 row than the more forgiving V3 row this file originally checked alone. Dropping the h1 to 4 lines (≈132px, saving ≈33px) brings the estimate to about 622px, which would clear V3 (18px of headroom) but still misses V2 by about 17px; closing that gap needs a further reduction (tighter door padding, a shorter provenance gap, or a narrower scent line) this estimate does not resolve. **The actual pass or fail is measured on the fixed copy with the QA runner at V2 and V3 (`17` section 4), not by this arithmetic** — this budget is a starting hypothesis for the design engineer, not a demonstrated fit.

Proximity ladder (F17), an **option (R15)** a brief may adopt, every value from the token scale: *within a door* `--space-3` 8px < *between the two doors* `--space-4` 16px < *between the door stack and the thesis* `--space-6` 32px < *between sections* `--space-8` 64px. Strictly increasing if adopted; a mock is free to use a different ladder provided it still reads as ordered grouping (P4).

Common region (F18): each door gets a **1px `--color-border` outline over a tint** (`--color-georgetown-tint` and `--color-anywhere-tint`), not a shadow. `--shadow-1` is reserved for chrome, as both live sites use it. This keeps the boundary strong enough to survive stacking without building A4's false floor.

### 5c. How sections should differ in weight (Option, R15)

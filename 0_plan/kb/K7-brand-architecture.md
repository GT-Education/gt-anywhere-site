## 1. Question

Three questions, in order.

1. **Architecture.** What are masterbrand, endorsed and house-of-brands systems, what decides between them, and how do schools with several campuses plus an online arm actually present themselves — naming, lockups, switchers, and how the parent brand endorses the children?
2. **Positioning.** What do the working frameworks (Dunford, Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass line, Aaker, Keller) say that a routing landing page must obey?
3. **Signalling.** How do selective institutions signal selectivity without wealth cues, and at what prominence is a network affiliation disclosed?

### 2a. The architecture frameworks

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Wally Olins is credited with the original three-structure typology of corporate identity — **monolithic, endorsed, branded** — in *The Corporate Personality* (1978) and *Corporate Identity* (1989). **Weaker provenance:** the publication list is from the fetched page; the typology itself reached me only through a search summary, not a direct fetch of Olins. Astra should re-verify before it is quoted anywhere outward-facing | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wally_Olins (fetched 2026-09-11; page last edited 2026-08-29). Typology via WebSearch summary, 2026-09-11 |
| F2 | Aaker and Joachimsthaler's **brand relationship spectrum** (California Management Review, 2000; expanded in *Brand Leadership*) runs from **branded house** through **sub-brands** and **endorsed brands** to **house of brands**. Branded house: "One master brand spans multiple offerings or businesses. Sub-names are typically descriptors of function or segment." House of brands: "A portfolio of stand-alone brands with minimal visible connection to the corporate owner" | https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/brand-relationship-spectrum-branded-house-to-house-of-brands/ (fetched 2026-09-11); corroborated at https://www.frmwrks.ai/library/brand-relationship-spectrum (fetched 2026-09-11) |
| F3 | The spectrum is decided by the **driver role**: "Which brand name most strongly drives purchase and user experience?" Secondary tests are equity transfer ("How much meaning should transfer across the portfolio?") and efficiency versus relevance | umbrex, as F2 |
| F4 | **Risk runs along the spectrum.** A branded house "increases spillovers risk"; a house of brands enables "risk containment". Independently: a branded house should be adopted only "when the reputation risk associated with different products is low, as one misstep could impact the entire company", and a house of brands "when there is a high reputation risk associated with certain products" | umbrex (as F2); Magda Adamska, BrandStruck, 24 Aug 2024, https://brandstruck.co/blog_post/brand-architecture-part-1-difference-branded-house-house-brands/ |
| F5 | **Endorsed brands** are the middle: "Independent product brands carry their own names, 'endorsed by' or visibly linked to the parent" — or, put the other way, "Independent brands backed by a credible parent". Sub-brands differ: there "the driver role is shared — sometimes master-led, sometimes co-driven" | umbrex and frmwrks.ai, as F2 |
| F6 | The generic three-level model: corporate/umbrella/family brand; endorsed brands and sub-brands, which include the parent "as an endorsement to a sub-brand or an individual, product brand"; and individual product brands, where "the parent company name is given little or no prominence" | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brand_architecture (fetched 2026-09-11; last edited 2026-07-29) |
| F7 | Keller's **brand hierarchy** orders elements as corporate → family → individual → modifier, with a *principle of simplicity*: beyond about three levels of naming, add family brands rather than depth. **Search-only provenance** (the primary text was not fetched); used only as corroboration of F6, never alone | WebSearch summary of Keller, *Strategic Brand Management*, 2026-09-11 |
| F8 | In higher education specifically: "a branded house is the preferred architecture" for efficiency, but it "is much easier said than done" given academic complexity, and "the brand relationship spectrum is a continuum, not the either/or proposition" practitioners usually frame. Example given: Arizona State chose "Arizona State University W. P. Carey School of Business" over a standalone identity | Rob Zinkan, Inside Higher Ed, 30 Nov 2016, https://www.insidehighered.com/blogs/call-action-marketing-and-communications-higher-education/branded-house-house-brands-or |

### 2b. Positioning frameworks

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F9 | "Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about." Positioning is "context-setting for products… a bit like the opening scene of a movie" | April Dunford, "An Introduction to Positioning", 4 May 2020, https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning |
| F10 | The five components, as questions: **Competitive alternatives** "What would customers do if our solution didn't exist?"; **Key unique attributes** "What do we have that the alternatives do not?"; **Value** "What is the value those capabilities enable for our buyers?"; **Target customers** "Who are the customers that care a lot about our value?"; **Market category** "The context we position our product in such that our value is obvious" | April Dunford, "A Quickstart Guide to Positioning", 15 Mar 2021, https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning |
| F11 | The category frame does the heavy lifting: "Your positioning context sets off a really powerful set of assumptions about who your product competes with, what features your product should have, who the product is intended for, and even things like what the product should cost." Her illustration: "If I were to tell you my product was 'email,' you would have a very different set of assumptions than if I positioned it as 'chat'" | Dunford, 2021 and 2020, as F9 and F10 |
| F12 | **Distinctiveness is not differentiation.** Differentiation is "features of a product or service that differentiate a brand in the eyes of the customer"; distinctiveness is "all about making your brand easily identified by customers. Brand names, logos, jingles, slogans and house styles contribute to this." The recommended posture: "Think 'Hey, we're here, we're good, give us a try!' rather than 'We are different from other brands, this is why you should buy us!'" | Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, "Differentiation versus distinctiveness", https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/differentiation-versus-distinctiveness (fetched 2026-09-11; no author or precise date on the page — it reports "3 years ago") |
| F13 | **Mental availability** "means that buyers easily think of the brand when buying and recognize it quickly." **Brand assets** are "all observable characteristics by which buyers can recognize brands and brand expressions", and should be "easily recognizable and eye-catching". **Category entry points** are the thoughts or motives that bring a buyer into the category, and "By connecting a brand to more category entry points, you increase the chance that it will be bought" | Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, "How do you measure How Brands Grow?", https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/how-do-you-measure-how-brands-grow (fetched 2026-09-11; undated, references Sharp's *How Brands Grow* and Romaniuk's *Better Brand Health*) |

### 2d. How the parent endorses the child: lockups, naming, prominence

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F31 | A **lockup** — not a separate logo — is the standard instrument: it "visually indicates the relationship between your unit and the larger university brand" by pairing the unit with the parent mark. Units are told not to make their own logos because that "leads to confusion and a diluted brand image", and "Not all departmental programs or activities should have a branded logo or lockup." Three levels: primary brand, primary sub-brands, secondary sub-brands (the third level names the parent unit too) | UC Berkeley, https://brand.berkeley.edu/visual-identity/signature-system/ (fetched 2026-09-11) |
| F32 | The rule is exclusive and mechanical: "units may only have **one** lockup ('logo'), which is created and maintained by University Communications"; "Lockups are the only permissible way to represent these groups with official trademarks"; "No other graphic, logo or designed permutations of the name that deviate from this construction may be used" | University at Buffalo, https://www.buffalo.edu/brand/identity/UB-visual-identity-system/sub-brands.html |
| F33 | A branded house stated as policy: "every logo, lockup, and identity in use across the enterprise must connect directly to the University of Rochester brand", chosen because it "creates recognition, clarity, and trust". Below schools and institutes, entities get **text-only treatments**, no lockups, "to prevent fragmentation" | University of Rochester, https://brand.rochester.edu/brand-architecture/ |
| F34 | A six-tier ladder that separates two things people conflate. A **sub-brand** "closely supports the university brand but has a mission that deviates" and "features its own logo with university branding in secondary position". An **individual brand** "expresses its connection to the university brand in name only". An **endorsed brand** "does **not** exist under the university brand" — the university grants permission to use its name for endorsement only | Virginia Tech, https://brand.vt.edu/architecture.html |
| F35 | Prominence is the regulated property of a disclosure, not a matter of taste: "Place it so it's hard to miss. The disclosure should be placed with the endorsement message itself"; "Don't mix your disclosure into a group of hashtags or links"; "Use simple and clear language"; and for images, "superimpose the disclosure over the picture and make sure viewers have enough time to notice and read it" | FTC, "Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers", Nov 2019, https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers |
| F36 | **Universal navigation** is the researched name for the switcher problem: it "provides a link to the main homepage of a website from subsites, sections, or microsites." Six guidelines, of which four bind here: include it on each subsite when several exist; **"Prioritize the subsite's navigation; don't allow universal navigation to compete for attention"**; place the main-site link in the upper left near the logo, labelled with the domain; **display universal categories only if users benefit from seeing scope or frequently switch between them** — and on mobile, de-emphasise them if they rarely do | Katie Sherwin, NN/g, 11 Sep 2016, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/universal-navigation/ |
| F37 | The older rule that still holds: a subsite is "a collection of Web pages within a larger site that have been given a common style and a shared navigation mechanism", and it "should definitely not aspire to become independent sites with no relation to the parent site." Each page links both to the subsite home and to the main home | Jakob Nielsen, NN/g, 31 Aug 1996, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-rise-of-the-subsite/ |
| F38 | Identity disclosure is expected at **four levels**: homepage tagline, an About summary, detailed sub-pages, footer links. "Users expect About Us sections to be clear, authentic, and transparent." Among the measured credibility positives: "Endorsements from reputable, external sources"; among the negatives, corporate jargon and stock photography | Anna Kaley and Jakob Nielsen, NN/g, 26 May 2019, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/ |
| F39 | **Ingredient branding** is the mechanism by which a shared component endorses many hosts: the component brands "go inside and aren't necessarily identifiable visually. Their quality message carries over to the brand they're inside of." Intel Inside (1991) is the canonical case | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingredient_branding (fetched 2026-09-11; last edited 2025-11-06) |

### 2e. How selective institutions signal selectivity without wealth cues

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F40 | **Selectivity stated as scarcity of seats, with the bar explicitly denied.** Stanford OHS: admission is "selective and by application only"; "Each year, we receive far more applicants than we have space to admit"; and, flatly, "Stanford OHS does not have a minimum test score or specific eligibility requirement." Students are described by disposition — "Intellectually Curious", "Globally-Conscious", "Growth-Minded" | https://onlinehighschool.stanford.edu/admissions |
| F41 | **Selectivity stated as a published bar.** Davidson Academy Online: "Profoundly gifted students are those who score in the 99.9th percentile on IQ and achievement tests." The number *is* the positioning | https://www.davidsononline.org/ |
| F42 | **Selectivity stated as an instrument, not a threshold.** Nueva names giftedness in its one-sentence self-description — "an independent PreK–12 school where gifted learners discover and develop their passions" — and lists "Grades PreK–8: IQ Assessment" as a step in admissions, with no threshold and no acceptance rate anywhere | https://www.nuevaschool.org/ |
| F43 | **Selectivity refused, access as the headline.** Success Academy leads with "Free and Expansive Programming" and itemises what costs nothing (supplies, instruments, field trips, uniforms, iPads), while stating the lottery and the prohibition on any admissions screen (F22) | https://www.successacademies.org/ |
| F44 | **Access published as a ladder, not a discount.** Khan World School @ ASU Prep states four prices by residency on one surface: Arizona residents at no cost; Arizona ESA/scholarship "$8,250 each year"; other U.S. students "$10,500 per year"; international "$13,700 per year" — all inclusive of two university courses a semester. No scarcity framing, no founding-family banner | https://asuprep.asu.edu/khan-world-school/ |
| F45 | **Why wealth cues are the wrong register for this audience.** Currid-Halkett: the "aspirational class" is "a well-to-do, educated elite" that "cements its status through prizing knowledge and building cultural capital", spending on "services, education and human-capital investments over purely material goods"; the top 1 percent spends nearly 6 percent of household expenditure on education against just over 1 percent for middle-income households. Education is itself the status good — so a page selling *education* gains nothing from displaying *wealth*, and loses the register that the people who value it most actually use | Elizabeth Currid-Halkett, Aeon, 7 Jun 2017, https://aeon.co/ideas/conspicuous-consumption-is-over-its-all-about-intangibles-now |
| F46 | **Ability framing as a selectivity proxy.** Crimson Global Academy: "At CGA, students are enrolled in classes based on ability, not age" and "Over 40% of our students are studying at an advanced level" — selectivity implied by what the cohort is doing, not by who was refused | https://www.crimsonglobalacademy.school/ |

**Reading of 2e.** Five institutions, five different instruments, and only one of them is a number. What they share is that **none signals selectivity with price, crest, architecture photography or scarcity**. Two of the three closest analogs to GT (Stanford OHS, Nueva) decline to publish a threshold; the third and closest (Davidson, a gifted school with a day school and an online school) publishes one. GT's own bar is already public (CR-011), which puts it in Davidson's column by continuity rather than by choice.

## 3. Principles the studio adopts

| # | Principle | Traces to |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | **Two architectures, two layers, never mixed on one surface.** Inside, GT School is a masterbrand over two named schools. Outside, GT School is an endorsed brand of the Alpha network. The landing expresses the inside layer with design (lockup, doors, switcher, shell) and the outside layer with **one sentence of prose** | F2, F5, F25, F34, F47, F52, F54 |
| P2 | **The driver gets the weight.** GT School drives the decision a parent is making on this page; Alpha only assures. Assurance never takes a mark, a colour, a position above the doors, or a second mention | F3, F5, F31, F35 |
| P3 | **Endorsement is a line, not a lockup.** A logo pair reads as co-drivers (F2) and imports the endorser's risk (F4); a sentence reads as provenance. Stanford OHS, Khan World School and Davidson all endorse in prose; the live Anywhere hero endorses with a mark, and that is the thing G0.5 changed | F2, F4, F25, F26, F34, F54 |
| P4 | **One mark, one lockup, no redrawn variants.** Real SVGs at their real proportions, on the surfaces `copy.md` already specifies. No recolouring, no re-typesetting, no invented "GT School Georgetown" mark | F31, F32, F33 |
| P5 | **Distinctiveness before differentiation.** The job of the marks, the palette and the two-door shape is to be *recognised* as GT, instantly, by someone arriving from an Instagram ad or an alpha.school link — not to argue that GT is different | F12, F13 |

## 5. How it applies to GT

### 5a. Which architecture GT is

Stated precisely, in the spectrum's own vocabulary:

> **Inside: a masterbrand with two co-driver sub-brands. Outside: a token-endorsed brand of the Alpha School network.**

Two layers, and the words matter. "Co-driver" rather than "descriptor", because "GT Anywhere" carries its own campaign and organic equity (DD-27, `10`) — it is not "GT School Online". "Token endorsement" rather than "strong endorsement", because G0.5 chose one small line once; the live site currently runs a *strong* endorsement (F54), and the redesign is a **downgrade along the spectrum**, which is a design decision with a name.
